PRE-OWNED HEAVY EQUIPMENT

Buying used earthmoving equipment in Australia can make sense for contractors, landscapers, farmers, civil operators and businesses expanding a machinery fleet. But the purchase should be based on the machine's actual condition, application, operating hours, maintenance history and total cost of ownership — not just the advertised price.

A good used machine can be a productive business asset. The challenge is knowing what to inspect, which specifications matter for your job and what questions to ask before money changes hands.

Used Excavators: What Should You Check?

Excavators are among the most commonly considered machines in the used earthmoving market. Before buying, look at more than the age and hour meter.

Operating hours

Hours provide useful context, but they do not tell the whole story. A machine's maintenance history, previous application, operating environment and service quality can be equally important.

Hydraulic system

Inspect for leaks, unusual noises, damaged hoses and inconsistent hydraulic performance. Ask whether the machine has been tested under load where practical.

Undercarriage

On tracked excavators, inspect tracks, rollers, idlers, sprockets and related components. Wear in the undercarriage can become a significant expense, so its condition should form part of your buying calculation.

Engine and cooling system

Look for evidence of leaks, excessive smoke, overheating history and poor maintenance. Check fluid levels and service records where available.

Choosing the Right Machine for the Job

Start with the work rather than the machine. The correct size depends on digging depth, lifting requirements, ground conditions, access, transport and the attachments you intend to use.

  • Landscaping: compact excavators and loaders can be useful where access is limited.
  • Trenching: consider digging depth, bucket size and hydraulic performance.
  • Construction: match machine capacity and attachments to the scale of work.
  • Agriculture: consider versatility, transport and attachment compatibility.
  • Civil work: larger equipment may be appropriate where production and cycle time are priorities.

Do not automatically assume that a larger machine is better. An oversized machine can increase transport and operating costs and may be impractical on smaller sites.

Buying Used Machinery: Price vs Total Cost

The purchase price is only one part of the cost of owning earthmoving equipment. Before comparing two machines, think about servicing, tyres or tracks, fuel, transport, attachments, insurance, finance costs and potential downtime.

A lower-priced machine that immediately needs major repairs may cost more overall than a better-maintained machine with a higher purchase price. That is why condition and intended use should be considered alongside the advertised price.

A used-machine warranty is different from buying a new machine, so read the actual terms applying to the particular machine and sales agreement.
